TOPIC 01 / 05
Topic One

Business & its
environment.

The five chapters that ground everything else: enterprise, structure, size, objectives, and stakeholders.

CH 1Enterprise

Purpose of business activity

Businesses exist to add value to raw materials and semi-finished goods to satisfy consumer needs and wants. This activity raises living standards by creating employment.

The four factors of production

  1. Land — natural resources used in production. Return: rent.
  2. Labour — manual and skilled work. Return: wages/salary.
  3. Capital — finance and man-made goods like machinery. Return: interest.
  4. Enterprise — the driving force that arranges the other three and bears risk. Return: profit.

Added value

added value = selling price − cost of bought-in materials

Increase added value by either raising selling price (better quality, advertising, packaging, USP) or lowering cost (lean production, cheaper supplies, training, automation).

Why new businesses fail

Internal problems
  • Weak business idea
  • Lack of managerial / entrepreneurial skills
  • Insufficient finance
  • Poor cash-flow management
  • Poor initial market research
External problems
  • Anticipated customers didn't materialise
  • Spending patterns changed
  • Unexpected competition
  • Economic / legal / technological shifts

Entrepreneur vs intrapreneur

EntrepreneurIntrapreneur
PurposeSet up a new businessInnovate within an existing one
Risk falls onThe entrepreneur personallyThe business (reputation, investment)
RewardProfitPromotion, pay rise, share of profit
Key qualitiesInnovative, self-motivated, leadership, resiliencePassionate, resourceful, independent, proactive

CH 2Business Structure

Sectors of activity

  • Primary — extracting natural resources (farming, fishing, mining)
  • Secondary — manufacturing and processing (cars, clothes, construction)
  • Tertiary — services (banking, retail, transport, tourism)
  • Quaternary — knowledge services (computing, R&D, consultancy)

Industrialisation vs de-industrialisation

Industrialisation (developing economies)

Secondary sector grows. Raises GDP, increases employment, boosts exports — but causes urbanisation pressures and import dependency.

De-industrialisation (developed economies)

Secondary sector shrinks as services grow. Rising incomes shift demand toward services; cheap manufactured imports replace domestic output.

Examiner reminder

Limited liability is the single most important advantage of incorporation. It means shareholders can only lose what they invested — never their personal wealth.

CH 3Size of Business

Five ways to measure size

  1. Number of employees — distorted by capital-intensive firms
  2. Revenue — useful within an industry, less so across
  3. Capital employed — long-term finance invested
  4. Market capitalisation — share price × shares issued (PLCs only)
  5. Market share — firm's sales as % of total market

Internal vs external growth

Internal (organic)

Opening new branches, increasing investment. Cheap, avoids takeover problems — but slow.

External (M&A)

Merger or takeover. Faster, gives instant scale — but risks culture clash and diseconomies.

Types of integration

TypeWhat it joinsWhy do it
HorizontalSame industry, same stageRemove a competitor, gain scale
Vertical forwardSame industry, customer sideControl distribution and pricing
Vertical backwardSame industry, supplier sideSecure inputs, control quality
ConglomerateDifferent industryDiversify risk across markets

CH 4Business Objectives

The hierarchy of objectives

Aim → Mission → Corporate objectives → Divisional objectives → Departmental objectives → Individual targets. Each level translates the one above into something more concrete and measurable.

SMART criteria

Specific · Measurable · Achievable · Realistic · Time-specific. Every objective should pass all five tests.

Common corporate objectives

  • Profit maximisation — output where TR – TC is greatest
  • Profit satisficing — enough profit to keep owners content
  • Growth — measured by sales / output, defends against takeover
  • Market share — being the brand leader brings retailer power
  • Survival — typical for start-ups
  • CSR — taking responsibility for impact on society and environment

The triple bottom line

Used especially by social enterprises: Economic · Social · Environmental. A business is judged not only by financial return but by its contribution to people and planet.

CH 5Stakeholders

A stakeholder is any person or group with an interest in a business's activity. The stakeholder concept argues that managers have responsibilities to many groups — not only shareholders.

StakeholderKey interest
CustomersQuality, value, safety, service
EmployeesPay, conditions, security, development
SuppliersOn-time payment, ongoing orders
ShareholdersDividends, share-price growth
LendersRepayment, interest, security
GovernmentTax revenue, employment, legal compliance
Local communityJobs, environment, infrastructure
Pressure groupsEthical, social, environmental conduct
Conflict resolution

Stakeholder interests often conflict — higher wages cut into profits; expansion disturbs neighbours. Managers prioritise, weigh costs, and assess publicity impact. Balancing these competing claims is a key reason senior pay is high.

Human resource
management.

People are the asset every theory in this topic tries to get right — from Taylor's stopwatch to McGregor's attitudes.

CH 10Human Resource Management

HRM is the strategic approach to managing people so they help the business gain competitive advantage. It covers planning, recruitment, development, contracts, morale, incentives, monitoring, and dismissal.

Recruitment process

  1. Job analysis — identify the vacancy and what it involves
  2. Job description — list of tasks, duties and responsibilities
  3. Person specification — qualities, skills, qualifications needed
  4. Job advertisement — internal or external
  5. Shortlisting — based on CVs, references, applications
  6. Selection — interview, aptitude tests, trial work

Internal vs external recruitment

Internal — promoting within
  • Cheaper, quicker, no induction
  • Already known, fits the culture
  • Motivates by showing progression
External — hiring outside
  • Brings new ideas, wider talent pool
  • Avoids internal jealousy
  • Higher standard of applicants possible

Training types

  • Induction — for all new recruits, covers systems and layout
  • On-the-job — at workplace, watching experienced staff. Cheap.
  • Off-the-job — away from work, with experts. Expensive but more thorough.

Labour turnover

labour turnover (%) = leavers in 1 year ÷ average employees × 100

CH 11Motivation Theories

F. W. Taylor — Scientific Management

Workers motivated only by money. Use piece-rate pay, autocratic style, close supervision. Time-and-motion studies find the one best way; everyone is trained to do it.

Limitation: ignores intrinsic motivation, encourages quantity over quality.

Elton Mayo — Hawthorne Effect

Productivity depends on social factors more than physical conditions. Talking with workers, team spirit, and giving control all raise output. Foundation of HR as a discipline.

Maslow — Hierarchy of Needs

  1. Physical (food, shelter)
  2. Safety (job security, health)
  3. Social (friendship, teamwork)
  4. Esteem (status, recognition)
  5. Self-actualisation (full potential)

Once a need is met, only the next level motivates further.

Herzberg — Two-Factor Theory

Hygiene factors

Pay, conditions, supervision. Their absence demotivates — their presence doesn't motivate.

Motivators

Achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement. These genuinely motivate.

McClelland — Three Motivational Needs

  • Achievement — set realistic goals, seek enrichment
  • Authority — desire to influence and lead
  • Affiliation — value friendly relations and teamwork

Vroom — Expectancy Theory

Motivation requires three beliefs simultaneously:

  1. Expectancy — effort will lead to performance
  2. Instrumentality — performance will lead to reward
  3. Valence — the reward is worth having

Financial methods of motivation

MethodHow it works
Time-ratePer hour worked
Piece-ratePer unit produced
SalaryAnnual income, paid monthly

Performance-related payReward for above-average performance

Non-financial methods

  • Job rotation — switch tasks to add variety
  • Job enlargement — broaden tasks (horizontal)
  • Job enrichment — deeper, more challenging work (Herzberg)
  • Empowerment — authority and resources to decide
  • Quality circles — voluntary problem-solving groups (Kaizen)
  • Team working — complete units of work as a group
  • Target setting — Management by Objectives

CH 12Management & Leadership

Functions of management (Fayol-style)

  1. Setting objectives and planning
  2. Organising resources to meet them
  3. Directing and motivating staff
  4. Coordinating activities across departments
  5. Controlling and measuring performance

Leadership styles

StyleDecisionsBest when…
AutocraticLeader aloneCrisis; unskilled workforce; speed needed
DemocraticShared with workforceSkilled team; complex decisions; time available
PaternalisticLeader, with consultationLoyal workforce expecting guidance
Laissez-faireDelegated to workersHighly skilled, self-motivated professionals

McGregor's Theory X & Y

Theory X — workers are lazy, dislike work, need control and direction.

Theory Y — workers are self-motivated, enjoy responsibility, seek fulfilment.

Crucially: these are management opinions about workers — not facts about them. Managers who hold Theory X tend to create Theory X behaviour.

Trade unions & industrial action

A trade union is a workers' organisation that negotiates pay and conditions through collective bargaining. When agreement fails, escalation can include go-slow, work-to-rule, overtime ban, and ultimately strike action.

Topic Three

Marketing.

Identify, anticipate, satisfy — at a profit. Four Ps, one mix, and a product life cycle that determines almost everything.

CH 17Nature of Marketing

Marketing is the management process responsible for identifying, anticipating, and satisfying consumers' requirements profitably. It coordinates with finance (budgets, cash flow), HR (workforce planning), and operations (capacity, NPD).

Demand & supply curves

Demand: quantity buyers will buy at a given price in a time period. Supply: quantity producers will offer. They meet at the equilibrium price. See the interactive chart on the Graphs page.

Mass vs niche marketing

Mass marketing

Same product, whole market. Wide choice, economies of scale, lower risk — but vulnerable to changes in taste and competing on price compresses image.

Niche marketing

Small segment, customised offer. Higher prices, stronger loyalty — but limited growth and vulnerable if the niche shifts.

Market segmentation

Splitting a market into sub-groups (age, income, gender, location, social class) and targeting each differently. Enables price discrimination, focused promotion, and identification of gaps.

CH 18Market Research

Primary vs secondary

Primary (field)Secondary (desk)
WhatFirst-hand data collected for purposeExisting data from elsewhere
MethodsQuestionnaires, interviews, observation, focus groups, test marketingGovernment data, trade reports, internet, internal records
ProUp-to-date, relevant, confidentialCheap, fast, broad coverage
ConExpensive, slow, sample biasMay be outdated or irrelevant

Statistical measures

  • Mean — totals ÷ count. Affected by outliers.
  • Mode — most frequent value. Useful for stocking decisions.
  • Median — middle value when ordered. Resistant to outliers.
  • Range — highest minus lowest. Shows spread.

CH 20Promotion & Place

Above-the-line: Advertising

Persuasive (build image) or informative (give facts). Choose media by cost-per-target-consumer, audience profile, message complexity, legal constraints.

Below-the-line: Sales promotion

  • Price reductions and money-off coupons
  • Customer loyalty schemes (cards, points)
  • BOGOF (buy one get one free)
  • Money-back refunds
  • Point-of-sale displays

Direct & digital promotion

  • Personal selling — one-to-one, costly, high success
  • Telemarketing — cheaper than personal but easily ignored
  • Direct mail — focused but easily missed
  • PR / sponsorship — free media coverage, reputation
  • Social media, email, SEO, influencers — measurable, targetable, global

Channels of distribution

  1. Manufacturer → Consumer (direct, online)
  2. Manufacturer → Retailer → Consumer
  3. Manufacturer → Wholesaler → Retailer → Consumer

Each layer adds margin and reach but reduces control. The right channel depends on the product type, market spread, and target audience.

Integrated marketing mix

The four Ps must reinforce each other. An exclusive product with cheap packaging at a discount price in a low-end shop sends contradictory signals — and contradictory signals don't sell.

Operations
management.

Inputs become outputs. The transformation process — and how efficiently, flexibly, and sustainably it runs.

CH 23The Nature of Operations

The transformational process

Inputs (enterprise, land, labour, capital) → transformation → outputs (goods, services, components). The job of operations management is to add value at every stage.

Productivity vs production

labour productivity = total output ÷ total workers

Production is total output. Productivity is output per unit of input. Productivity drives unit cost down — production is just volume.

Production methods

MethodWhat it makesStrengthsWeaknesses
JobOne-off bespoke itemsCustomised, premium pricesHigh cost, slow, skilled labour
BatchGroups of identical itemsSome scale, flexible varietyStorage of WIP, downtime between batches
FlowContinuous mass productionBig economies of scale, consistent qualityHigh capital, low job satisfaction
Mass customisationCustom at scale (CAM/CAD)Tailored at low unit costTech investment, complex coordination

Economies & diseconomies of scale

Economies of scale
  • Purchasing — bulk discounts
  • Technical — fixed costs spread
  • Financial — better loan terms
  • Marketing — promotion spread over output
  • Managerial — afford specialists
Diseconomies of scale
  • Communication — long chains, distortion
  • Alienation — workers feel unimportant
  • Coordination — hard to align departments

Sustainability

Operations that can be maintained long-term without depleting resources or harming future generations. Done through recycled inputs, waste reduction, lower energy use, sustainable suppliers.

CH 24Inventory Management

Three types of inventory

  • Raw materials — purchased from suppliers
  • Work in progress — partly converted
  • Finished goods — ready for sale

Costs of holding vs not holding

Holding costs
  • Opportunity cost of capital tied up
  • Storage, security, insurance
  • Risk of damage or obsolescence
Not holding (stockouts)
  • Lost sales
  • Idle production resources
  • Expensive emergency orders
  • Loss of bulk discounts

JIT vs JIC

Just-in-Time (JIT)

Inventory arrives as needed. Frees capital, reduces obsolescence — but vulnerable to supply disruption and requires excellent supplier relations.

Just-in-Case (JIC)

Hold high buffer stocks. Production never stops, bulk discounts apply — but capital is tied up and obsolescence risk grows.

CH 25Capacity & Outsourcing

Capacity utilisation

capacity utilisation (%) = current output ÷ maximum output × 100

Operating at maximum capacity

Benefits
  • Lowest possible average fixed cost
  • Job security, employee pride
  • Marketing benefit — visible demand
Drawbacks
  • Worker stress, no slack time
  • Maintenance is delayed
  • Cannot meet additional orders
  • Faster machine depreciation

Outsourcing

Using a third party to undertake part of the production process. Cuts costs, adds flexibility, frees focus for core activities — but reduces quality control, raises ethical questions, and creates dependence.

CH 29Business Finance

Why businesses need finance

  • Start-up capital — buy initial assets
  • Working capital — fund day-to-day operations
  • Expansion, acquisitions, R&D
  • Cover unforeseen expenses

Two types of expenditure

  • Capital expenditure — long-term, on assets lasting >1 year (machinery, buildings)
  • Revenue expenditure — short-term, day-to-day (wages, materials, utilities)

Working capital

working capital = current assets − current liabilities

Too little: insolvency risk. Too much: opportunity cost of idle cash. Manage it through inventory control, credit policy, payment timing.

Debt vs equity

Debt finance

Ownership stays intact, interest is tax-deductible, lenders have no votes — but loans must be repaid, with collateral.

Equity finance

Never repaid, no interest pressure — but dilutes ownership, dividends paid from profit, control may shift.

CH 30Cash Flow

Cash flow is the sum of cash inflows minus outflows. A profitable business can fail through poor cash flow — profit is a long-term goal, but cash is the short-term oxygen.

Cash flow forecast structure

LineWhat it means
Opening balanceCash held at start of month
Cash inflowsSales, capital, loans, receivables
Cash outflowsSuppliers, wages, rent, marketing, equipment
Net cash flowInflows − outflows
Closing balanceOpening + net flow → next month's opening

Causes of cash flow problems

  • Lack of planning
  • Poor credit control (slow trade receivables, bad debt)
  • Overtrading — expanding without funding
  • Unforeseen costs or demand swings

Improving cash flow

Boost inflows
  • Overdraft, short-term loan
  • Sale or sale-and-leaseback of assets
  • Debt factoring
  • Tighter credit period for customers
  • Discount for prompt payment
Slow outflows
  • Delay capital expenditure
  • Lease rather than buy
  • Cut overheads
  • Extend credit period from suppliers
  • Buy more on credit, less in cash

CH 31Costs

Cost classification

  • Direct — identifiable with a single unit of output (raw materials)
  • Indirect (overheads) — cannot be allocated to one unit (rent, admin)
  • Fixed — don't vary with output in the short run
  • Variable — vary directly with output
  • Marginal — cost of one extra unit

Costing methods

MethodAllocatesBest for
Full (absorption)All direct + indirect costsPricing, profit per product, single-product firms
MarginalVariable cost onlyShort-term decisions, special orders
ContributionDirect cost; rest covered by contributionMulti-product mix decisions, break-even

Break-even analysis

contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit
break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit
margin of safety = actual sales − break-even sales

Explore break-even visually on the Graphs page — change costs and prices and see how the break-even point moves.

Budgets & variance

  • Incremental budgeting — last year's budget plus an adjustment
  • Zero-based budgeting — start from zero, justify every line
  • Flexible budgeting — vary cost budgets as sales vary
  • Variance analysis — comparison of budgeted vs actual

Favourable variance: actual outcome leads to higher profit than budgeted. Adverse variance: actual outcome leads to lower profit than budgeted.
